# figures/

| file | what it shows | why it exists |
|---|---|---|
| `calibration.png` | corpus score distribution + the 125 blind labels | the honest picture of what the metric can and cannot separate |
| `mass_letter_top.png` | the 24 highest-scoring windows, score burned in | if the top of the ranking is not visibly readable, the metric is wrong and the atlas means nothing |
| `mass_letter_bottom.png` | the 24 lowest-scoring windows | the same test in the other direction |
| `mass_letter_median.png` | 24 windows from the middle of the distribution | where the metric is weakest, shown rather than hidden |
| `mass_letter_ladder.png` | one row of samples per score decile | turns a ranking into a threshold: it shows *where along the range* text starts appearing. It is also the figure that proves the metric is **not** visually monotonic in the bulk |
| `maps/` | per-segment maps: prediction on top, score field below, same geometry | a percentage says how much of a segment is readable, not *where* |

Contact sheets carry the score on every tile so they can be checked one by one — the point
is to make the metric falsifiable by eye, not to look impressive.

The maps are a curated selection (the 10 best and 6 worst ranked predictions). All 74 are
regenerated by `scripts/segment_map.py` from `data/atlas.csv` plus the ds8 cache. Blending
the score into the prediction as a tint was tried first and abandoned: a tint heavy enough
to read as a score washes out the very text it is scoring, so the two are stacked instead.
White contours mark the regions above the calibrated threshold.
